\begin{solution}{ex:em-1}
  (a)
  Using Coulomb's law, we have

  \[\abs{F}=k \frac{\abs{q_1q_2}}{r^2}\]
  The force on $q_3$ due to $q_1$:

  \[\abs{F_{31}}=k \frac{10.0 \cdot 10^{-12} C}{0.15m} \approx 0.6N\]
  The direction is away from $q_1$, since they are both positively charged. The force on $q_3$ due to $q_2$:

  \[
    \abs{F_{32}}=k \frac{15.0 \cdot 10^{-12} C}{0.25cm} \approx 0.54 N 
  \]
  This time the force points towards $q_2$ since they are oppositely charged.\\\\
  (b)
  The net force is just the sum of the previous 2 forces in this case.
  \[\overrightarrow{F_{net}}=\overrightarrow{F_{31}}+\overrightarrow{F_{32}}\]
  It is clear that $\overrightarrow{F_{32}}$ and $\overrightarrow{F_{31}}$ both point in the positive x-axis direction. Thus:
  \[
  \begin{gathered}
        F_{net}=F_{31}+F_{32}\\
        F_{net}=0.6N+0.54N=1.14N
  \end{gathered}
\]
  (c)
  The electric field is given by:
  \[
    E=\frac{F_{net}}{q_0}
  \]
  We have the following equation:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      E&=0\\
      \frac{F_{net}}{q_0}&=0\\
      \frac{\frac{q_1q_0}{x^2}+\frac{q_2q_0}{(40-x)^2}}{q_0}&=0\\
      \frac{q_1}{x^2}+\frac{q_2}{(40-x)^2}&=0\\
      \frac{(40-x)^2q_1}{x^2}+q_2&=0\\
      \frac{(40-x)^2}{x^2}&=-\frac{q_2}{q_1}\\
      \frac{40-x}{x}&=\sqrt{-\frac{q_2}{q_1}}\\
      40-x&=x\sqrt{-\frac{q_2}{q_1}}\\
      40&=x(1+\sqrt{-\frac{q_2}{q_1}})\\
      x&=\frac{40}{1+\sqrt{-\frac{q_2}{q_1}}}\\
      x&\approx18cm
    \end{aligned}
  \]
\end{solution}

      v=\sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot 400V \cdot e}{m_e}}\\
      v\approx 11.8 \cdot 10^{12} m/s
    \end{gathered}
  \]

\begin{solution}{ex:em-3}
  (a) Its capacitance is given by:
  \[
    C=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r \frac{A}{d}
  \]
  We'll use $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \cdot 10^{-12} F/m$, $\varepsilon_r = 4.0 F/m$, $A=0.005 m^2$ and $d=0.005 m$. We have:

    \end{gathered}

  \[
    Q=CU
  \]
  So using the capacitance obtained earlier we have:
  \[
    Q\approx8.5 \cdot 10^{-10} C
  \]
  (c) The battery is disconnected, let's treat Q as fixed and U as variable. The capacitance is half of what it used to be, since the separation is doubled. We have:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      C_f=1.75 \cdot 10^{-11} F = \frac{C}{2}\\
      U_f=\frac{Q}{C_f}=48 V

  \[
    W_f=\frac{1}{2}C_fU_f^2\approx2.03 \cdot 10^{-8} J

\begin{solution}{ex:em-4}
  (a) The total resistance is given by:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      R_t=R_1+R_{23}\\
      R_t=R_1+\frac{R_2R_3}{R_2+R_3}\\
      R_t=282 \Omega
    \end{gathered}
  \]
  The current is given by:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      I_{total}=\frac{U_{total}}{R_{total}}\\
      I_{total}=\frac{12V}{282\Omega}\approx42mA
    \end{gathered}
  \]

  (b) For the whole circuit, we can think of the resistor pair $R_{23}$ as one single resistor. Then, we see that $R_1$ and $R_{23}$ are wired in series. We know that the voltage divides between resistors in series in proportion to their resistance. The voltage across $R_1$ is then:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      U_1=U_{total}\frac{R_1}{R_t}\\
      U_1=12V \frac{150\Omega}{282\Omega}\\
      U_1\approx6.4V
    \end{gathered}
  \]
    Then for the pair $R_{23}$, we have:
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
        U_{23}=U_{total}\frac{R_{23}}{R_t}\\
        U_{23}\approx5.6V
      \end{gathered}
    \]
    For the resistors $R_2$ and $R_3$, the voltage stays the same ($5.6V$), but the current gets divided in proportion to the conductance ($G=\frac{1}{R}$):
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
        I_{23}=I_{total} \qquad\text{(Since $R_1$ and $R_{23}$ are wired in series)}\\
        I_{2}=I_{23} \frac{\frac{1}{R_2}}{\frac{1}{R_2}+\frac{1}{R_3}}\\
        I_{2}=I_{23} \frac{R_{23}}{R_2}\\
        I_2\approx 25 mA\\\\
        I_3=I_{23}\frac{R_{23}}{R_3}\\
        I_3\approx17mA
      \end{gathered}
    \]
    (c)
    The power dissipated in $R_1$ is given by:
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
        P_1=U_1I_1\\
        P_1=6.4V\cdot 42mA\\
        P_1=0.27W
      \end{gathered}
    \]
    The total power given by the battery is given by:
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
      P_{total}=U_{total}I_{total}\\
      P_{total}=12V \cdot 42mA\\
      P_{total}=0.5W
      \end{gathered}
    \]
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:em-5}
  (a)
  Let's choose that the field points to the left (negative x), and that the proton is going forward (positive y). The z-axis points up. Using the right hand rule (middle finger represents the field, index finger represents the velocity and the thumb represents the resulting force), we deduce that the initial force vector points upwards, towards the z-axis.
  (b) The magnitude of the force is given by:
  \[
    \abs{F}=qvB \qquad\text{(Since the velocity is perpendicular to the field)}
  \]
  Since the proton is perpendicular to the field, $F$ is a centripetal force and the proton traces a circular path. Following the equations for circular motion:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      a=\frac{v^2}{r}\\
      F=\frac{mv^2}{r}\\
      qvB=\frac{mv^2}{r}\\
      r=\frac{mv^2}{qvB}\\
      r=\frac{mv}{qB}\\
      r\approx0.013m=13mm
    \end{gathered}
  \]
  The period is just the circumference divided by the velocity:
  \[
    T=\frac{2\pi r}{v}\approx2.6\cdot10^{-7}s
  \]
  (c) For an electron, the equations don't change, except that it has an equal and opposite charge compared to a proton. Then, the force will point in the opposite direction (negative z) and thus the circular motion will be the same but in the opposite direction.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:em-6}
  Knowing that $\overrightarrow{F}=q\overrightarrow{v}\times\overrightarrow{B}$ already tells us a crucial detail: the force exerted on a particle in a magnetic field is always perpendicular to the velocity. Let's see the definition of mechanical work:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      W =\int \overrightarrow{F}\cdot ds\\
      W =\int \overrightarrow{F} \cdot \overrightarrow{v} dt\\
      W =\int (q \overrightarrow{v}\times \overrightarrow{B}) \cdot \overrightarrow{v} dt \\
      W =q\int(\overrightarrow{v}\times \overrightarrow{B}) \cdot \overrightarrow{v} dt \\
    \end{gathered}
  \]
  The integrand is now a scalar triple product. A crucial identity of the scalar triple product is that $\overrightarrow{a}\cdot(\overrightarrow{a}\times \overrightarrow{b})=0$. Thus, the integral evaluates to 0 and the work done is also 0.
  From this we can deduce that the mechanical energy is conserved. Furthermore, since the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, we know that the magnitude of the velocity (speed) is also constant, and from this follows that the kinetic energy is also constant.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:em-7}
  Each charge carrier experiences a force from the magnetic field given by:
  \[
     \overrightarrow{F}=q \overrightarrow{v}\times\overrightarrow{B}
  \]
  These forces apply to each electron so the charges build up to one end of the rod. They will then achieve an equilibrium once the electric field they build up cancels out the aforementioned force, so:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      qE=q \overrightarrow{v} \times \overrightarrow{B}\\
      qE=qvB \qquad \text{(Since the magnetic field is perpendicular to the velocity)}\\
      E=vB
    \end{gathered}
  \]
  To get the tension, we just need the difference in potential, which scales linearly in proportion to the length:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      U_{ind}=LE\\
      U_{ind}=BLv
    \end{gathered}
  \]
  There's also a more simple explanation for the formula. If we take the whole circuit as the coil, then its flux changes in proportion to its area. The area grows $Lv$ each second, so the flux in this scenario grows $BLv$ each second which is exactly the induced voltage. This formula holds when the magnetic field is perpendicular to the circuit and the velocity of the rod. Otherwise we'd have to take cross products for the velocity and dot products for the coil.\\
  (b) Using the aforementioned formula, the induced voltage is:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      U_{ind}=BLv\\
      U_{ind}=0.2 T \cdot 0.5 m \cdot 4.0 m/s\\
      U_{ind}=0.4 V
    \end{gathered}
  \]
  The induced current can be calculated using the relation $I=\frac{U}{R}$:
  \[
    I_{ind}=\frac{0.4 V}{5.0 \Omega}=80 mA
  \]
  Since the uniform magnetic field points up, the induced magnetic field must point down. Using the right hand rule (curling the fingers in the induced magnetic field gives us the direction of current), we can deduce that the current must flow counter-clockwise. (The electrons flow in the opposite direction).\\
  (c) Since the magnetic field doesn't do any work, we can assert that energy is conserved in the system:
  \[
    \begin{gathered}
      E_k+E_e=E_0\\
      \frac{1}{2}mv(t)^2+Pt=\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\\
      \frac{1}{2}mv(t)^2+UIt=\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\\
      \frac{1}{2}mv(t)^2+\frac{U^2}{R}t=\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\\
      \frac{1}{2}mv(t)^2+\frac{(BLv(t))^2}{R}t=\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\\
      v(t)^2(\frac{1}{2}m+\frac{(BL)^2t}{R})=\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\\
      v(t)^2=\frac{\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2}{\frac{1}{2}m+\frac{(BL)^2t}{R}}\\
      v(t)^2=\frac{mv_0^2}{m+\frac{2(BL)^2t}{R}}\\
      v(t)^2=\frac{Rmv_0^2}{Rm+2(BL)^2t}\\
      v(t)^2=\frac{Rmv_0^2}{Rm+2B^2L^2t}\\
      v(t)=\sqrt{\frac{Rmv_0^2}{Rm+2B^2L^2t}}
    \end{gathered}
  \]
    We observe that $v(t)$ is decreasing. To keep the velocity constant, we need to do work that is equal to the energy dissipated in the resistor.
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
        W=E_e\\
        W=UIt\\
        W=\frac{U^2t}{R}\\
        W=\frac{(BLv)^2t}{R}
      \end{gathered}
    \]
    Let's find the external force from the work:
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
        W=Fs\\
        Fs=\frac{(BLv)^2t}{R}\\
        Fvt=\frac{(BLv)^2t}{R}\\
        Fv=\frac{(BLv)^2}{R}\\
        F=\frac{(BL)^2v}{R}\\
      \end{gathered}
    \]
    Since the velocity stays constant in this scenario, we can calculate F:
    \[
        F=\frac{(0.2 T \cdot 0.5 m)^2 4.0 m/s}{5.0 \Omega}\\
        F=0.008N
    \]
    The power dissipated in the resistor is simply. 
    \[
      \begin{gathered}
        P=UI\\
        P=\frac{U^2}{R}\\
        P=\frac{(BLv)^2}{R}\\
        P=\frac{(0.2 T \cdot 0.5 m \cdot 4.0 m/s)^2}{5.0 \Omega}\\
        P= 0.032W = Fv
      \end{gathered}
    \]
\end{solution}
